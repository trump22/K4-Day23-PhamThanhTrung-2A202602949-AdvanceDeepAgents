# Survey of World Models: Foundations, Recent Advances, and Evaluation

## TL;DR

1. Modern 'world models' in AI are internal generative models of an environment, enabling planning and policy learning ([1]).
2. Recent innovations leverage foundation models, recursive rulebooks, and agent self-improvement cycles, broadening application beyond classical RL ([2],[3]).
3. Evaluation frameworks now prioritize long-horizon consistency, physical plausibility, and interpretability rather than just visual fidelity ([4],[5],[6]).
4. New agent-based diagnostics and rationalized benchmarks improve transparency, but scalability and generalization remain active challenges ([6],[7]).
5. Most experimental results are domain-specific—general world modeling and robust performance in unstructured settings are unsolved ([3],[4],[6]).

## Background

World models are internal constructs through which intelligent agents simulate, reason about, and plan within their environments. While the concept traces to cognitive "mental models," in artificial intelligence it solidified as the use of generative models—often neural networks—to learn compressed spatial and temporal representations ([1]). These models underpin model-based reinforcement learning (RL), where agents can train using synthetic experience generated by the model, reducing reliance on interactions with the real environment.

Ha & Schmidhuber’s 2018 paper formalized the neural world model, showing agents could learn not only to model environmental dynamics but also to improve behavior policies entirely within these simulated contexts ([1]). Since then, the idea has expanded into high-dimensional sensory domains, embodied robotics, and multi-agent systems, as well as into generative modeling outside of strict RL frameworks ([2],[3]).

## Foundational Theories and Early Frameworks

The earliest formalizations of world models in AI derived from cognitive science notions of mental models, as internal simulators supporting inference and planning. Ha & Schmidhuber’s work is often credited with establishing the deep learning-based world model, integrating variational autoencoders and recurrent networks to create compact compressed models of the environment’s spatial and temporal patterns ([1]). Their regime supports both hallucinated experience for policy learning and introspective capability (agents "dreaming" scenarios for improved generalization).

Early world-model frameworks focused on simulation and model-based policy optimization. The core principle is enabling agents to plan ahead by imagining possible action sequences, making model-based RL more sample efficient than classic model-free learning. The cognitive "mental model" perspective persists: world models act as stand-ins for physical reality in an agent’s cognitive map.

## Methodological Advances in World Models (2025–2026)

Recent years have seen a rapid expansion in world model research—both in scope and method. Four major innovation streams for large-scale or more capable world models are evidenced:

- **Foundation model integration in multi-agent RL**: Leveraging large pre-trained models (foundational models, FM) within decentralized multi-agent RL enables improved learning speed and scalability. For example, an FM-aided actor-critic approach outperformed standard methods for wireless network optimization ([2]).

- **Recursive self-improvement with interpretable rulebooks**: Techniques like Memento 3 use agents' persistent, natural language rulebooks as external semantic memory. Agents iterate observation, reflection, and rulebook/code revision, using explicit error-driven cycles for model improvement. This enhances interpretability while guiding agent exploration ([3]).


- **Open-ended agent self-improvement and exploration**: Studies with recursive and meta-reflection loops detail how agents update or revise their models and rulebooks to improve task coverage and reasoning (e.g., through supervision, error-driven updates, or meta-reflection) over baseline systems ([3]).

A common methodological theme is the movement from purely neural, opaque models toward more interpretable, modular, and self-improving systems ([3]). However, most such advances remain demonstrated on well-defined, contrived environments—generalization remains challenging.

## Evaluation Strategies and Limitations

World model evaluation itself has become a field of innovation. Whereas early benchmarks emphasized visual realism or next-step prediction, current frameworks assess broader functional fidelity: long-horizon behavioral consistency, physical plausibility, and appropriateness for downstream policy applications ([4],[5],[6]).

- **Policy evaluation with video world models**: For robotics, action-conditioned video generation models can reduce real-world testing needs, using metrics like policy ranking and predicted-vs-actual value correlations. However, sim-to-real gaps and domain transfer persist ([4]).

- **Multi-dimensional and agentified diagnostics**: New frameworks such as HarnessEval-W break down world model assessment into micro-tasks solved by specialized agents, constructing reasoning chains that justify each evaluation score. This approach aligns more closely with human judgments while improving transparency ([7]).

- **Long-horizon and physical/behavioral fidelity**: GigaWorld-1 highlights the importance of long-term rollout consistency and controllability, showing that these factors are better predictors of policy value than short-term visual quality ([4]). 4DWorldBench further expands by covering perceptual, geometric, and cross-modal consistency ([6]).

- **Explainable, human-aligned evaluation**: WorldLens includes textual rationales from distilled evaluation agents, encompassing not just surface visual plausibility but also deeper behavioral and functional reliability ([5]).

Despite these advances, evaluation tools are frequently limited to specific domains (robotics, driving, simulated environments) and rely heavily on either annotated datasets or skills of sub-agents for judgment ([6],[7]). Scalability of interpretability, consistency in uncurated environments, and generalization of validity all remain as open challenges.

## Interpretability, Generalization, and Open Problems

While new methods and diagnostics are moving world models toward greater transparency and autonomy, clear limitations remain:

- Most world model successes are demonstrated in highly curated settings (robotics, video games, well-specified simulation domains); true generalization and robustness to real-world complexities are unproven ([3],[4],[6]).
- Interpretability improvements (via rulebook recursion or agentified evaluation) have not yet scaled or been proven across the diversity of real-world contexts ([3],[7]).
- Heavy dependence on annotated data, sub-agent skills, or human-evaluated rationales persists; autonomous reliability is not fully achieved ([5],[6],[7]).
- While foundational models and reflective loops improve scalability, catastrophic forgetting, transfer learning, and explainability at scale remain hot research areas ([2],[3]).

## Trends and Open Problems

- There is movement from "black box" neural reasoning toward modular, interpretable, self-revising world models ([3],[7]).
- Robustness, generalization, and evaluation in truly open-world or sparsely labeled settings are largely unsolved ([3],[4],[6]).
- Integration of foundation models and language-centric world modeling is likely to expand, enabling policy learning and exploration as well as traditional planning ([2],[3]).
- Evaluation frameworks trend toward rationalized, multi-agent, and human-aligned approaches, yet scalability and objective validity in new domains remain key hurdles ([6],[7]).

Empirical progress is significant, and world models are now central to agent cognition and planning in several subfields, but their real-world robustness, adaptive generalization, and explainability outside narrow domains still pose major open questions.
